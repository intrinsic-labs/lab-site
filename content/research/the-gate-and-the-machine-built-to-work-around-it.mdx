Our company's operating record is a folder of Markdown files under git — one file per task, with an owner, and a scheduled process that hands agent-owned tasks to autonomous work sessions. In early August we added a requirement: a task could be agent-owned *and* unblocked *and* still not be picked up unless it also carried a `#delegate` tag applied by the human. The tag was a second consent, on top of the per-project permission level that already governed what a session was allowed to do.

This is the story of what that gate cost, of the machine we built to compensate for it, and of what happened when we deleted both.

## The gate produced a bottleneck, then a workaround

The tag had to be typed by hand, so it mostly wasn't. By 2026-08-19 an internal review recorded that near-zero tagging was "a measured bottleneck twice over" — the same finding had come up four days earlier. The queue that agents could see was almost empty while the queue that existed was not.

The fix chosen on 2026-08-19 was a scheduled classifier: every morning at 07:12 a session would nominate and tag up to five tasks per product line, post the list to a chat channel by 07:20, and the human could untag anything by replying with ids before the first work tick at 08:35. The review window was structural — an hour of clock, not a promise.

It ran for eight days. Its own logs say what it did:

- It fired on six mornings, tagged **23 tasks total**, and only ever touched **one project** — every run logged the other two as "no untagged agent work in the active section."
- One of six runs was a hard failure: the session exited non-zero having tagged nothing.
- **It received zero vetoes.** The reply-to-untag verb, the review window, the whole opt-out design: never exercised once.

And on day three an automated efficacy pass — a scheduled check comparing what a change was supposed to achieve against what actually happened — had already reported the central safety property broken: the chat post "has landed roughly four hours after the tick it's supposed to gate on both mornings it has run." The window that made an opt-out design safe had not existed on either morning, and nobody noticed for six more days.

## The measurement that killed both

On 2026-08-27 an audit session went through 16 automated review runs, 14 days of work-session logs and the whole tooling tree. Two numbers mattered.

**25 of 34 agent-owned tasks in one product were silenced for want of the tag.** Reproduced from the task file as it stood at 07:13 that morning: 34 open agent-owned lines, 14 carrying the tag. Restricted to the active section alone, which is the part that actually schedules, it was 15 of 29. Either way the gate was suppressing most of the eligible work in the project with the deepest queue.

**61% of 153 work sessions over 14 days were the system working on itself** — against 10% on client-facing work and zero sessions on two of the three product bets.

The second number reframes the first. The gate was not protecting anything the permission model wasn't already protecting — sensitive work was covered by per-project autonomy levels and by human ownership. What the gate did was keep the product queues empty while the one queue nobody had gated, the system's own, absorbed the spare capacity. The recorded decision, in the human's words: *"The #delegate thing is doing more harm than help."*

So, all on one day: the tag requirement was **inverted** (agent-owned tasks in the active section dispatch by default, in every category), a new tag `#mine` was introduced as a per-task reserve for work the human wants to keep, the morning classifier was **retired**, and a cap was added allowing at most one of a tick's three concurrent sessions to be the system working on itself.

## Why this shape and not the obvious alternative

The obvious alternative was on the table: keep the gate and tune the classifier's bar. It was rejected because the classifier existed only to work around the gate's friction — a grant ritual performed every morning by a scheduled model to compensate for a rule a human was never going to satisfy by hand. Tuning it would have been tuning the workaround.

## What it cost, and what actually happened

**It broke the prompts for a day.** A maintenance session found four live prompt files and one document still teaching the retired rule — including, in the prompt of the session that picks the work, a positive false claim about what was eligible. For roughly 24 hours the code enforced the new rule while the model choosing the work was told the old one. The audit's first finding was that the same fact living in five prose places guarantees drift.

**It exposed a second-order bug.** The widened pool immediately started re-nominating a task in a repository checkout that was parked and unusable — five ticks, five refusals, zero sessions for that product — because the nomination refresh consulted per-line eligibility only, never repository state.

**Half the intervention worked.** Counting work sessions from the dated run reports, by project category:

| window | sessions | product bets | client work | system self-work |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 08-13 → 08-26 (before) | 153 | 18% | 10% | **61%** |
| 08-27 → 09-03 (after) | 57 | 32% | 0% | **68%** |

Bets nearly doubled as a share, and one product that had received zero sessions in fourteen days got four in the two days after the change. That is the inversion doing what it was designed to do.

Self-work did not fall. It rose. And the cap meant to hold it down **has never fired once** — no log line naming it exists. Its own source comment predicted that: an existing rule, at most one session per project per tick, already made at most one self-work session possible, so the new cap "mostly restates an invariant that structure happens to provide." The audit had said the imbalance was *"inventory, upstream of dispatch"* — how many maintenance tasks get filed per day, not how many get scheduled. The intervention was applied at dispatch, so it could not have worked. The reserve tag has never been used either; as of 2026-09-03 no task carries it.

Client work went from 16 sessions to zero, consistent with the audit's note that all five client projects held four agent-runnable tasks between them. An empty hopper is not a gate, and eligibility changes cannot fill it.

## Where this goes next

The 2026-08-19 review set itself a falsifiable check: by roughly 2026-09-02, most work sessions should be product or client work rather than system self-work, and gated work should be merging in under a day. **That check was assigned to a weekly automated pass and was never run** — only two such passes exist in the record, neither computes session mix, and the cadence stopped after 08-28. The table above is, as far as the record shows, the first time anyone computed it. Against the first criterion the answer is no.

That is the finding under the finding. Setting a falsifiable bar is cheap; owning the job of reading it has to be assigned to something that actually runs.

So there are two concrete pieces of work, and they differ. The first is to move the intervention to where the audit said the imbalance lives: make self-filed maintenance tasks default to deferred unless something is actually broken, so the hopper stops filling itself. The second is to give the falsifiable check an owner that runs — the same table above, recomputed on a fixed cadence by something that fails loudly when it doesn't run, rather than by whoever thinks to ask. Gated-work merge latency still has no number and is the other half of that report.

What working looks like is unambiguous and already written down: most sessions on product or client work rather than on the system, and gated work merging in under a day. The share of sessions aimed outward is [published](/instruments/vault) for exactly this reason. It is the number to watch next, and the honest expectation is that it moves only when the client and product queues have something in them.
